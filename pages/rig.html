Why a rig

Walking around someone with a phone is the quick way to scan a person, and fine for a rough avatar. It isn't good enough for a dress form. The hand-held phone slowly loses track of exactly where it is, and the person sways while the phone is behind them with nothing to notice. Both errors end up in the shape.

The rig fixes both. The phones don't move, so their positions are measured once and stay exact. The person turns slowly on a turntable, so every side is seen, and two phones watching from different directions catch every lean and breath so Doppelmaker can take them out. How it works explains the reasoning in more depth.

Layout

Setting the heights, measured from the subject, never from the floor:

  • Phone A: lens at the navel. Its view ends about 38 cm below that on the body, around the upper thighs, so the hips are well inside it.
  • Phone B: lens 15–25 cm above the shoulders. The lens sits about 22 cm above the bottom of its cap (iPhone 17 Pro; 23.5 cm on a 17 Pro Max), so put the bottom of B's cap at shoulder height. Its view ends below the hips.
  • For phone A the same offset puts the cap about 22 cm below the navel.
  • Phone A faces their front, lens at about navel height, tilted up 10°.
  • Phone B is at their side, 90° around from A, lens 15–25 cm above their shoulders and tilted down 12°, so it can see the tops of the shoulders.
  • Both about a metre from the turntable centre (0.9–1.2 m), in portrait, with cases off, on right-angle USB-C chargers: LiDAR recording warms a phone.
  • Exact heights and angles don't matter, and neither the floor nor the turntable is ever measured: everything is relative to the phones themselves and to the subject. The calibration target measures where each phone is relative to the others.

A third phone

A third phone (any LiDAR iPhone, 12 Pro on) is used as soon as it has a role in Studio: no setting says where it stands, because calibration measures that. The layout worth trying is three phones about a metre out and 120° apart: A in front at navel height, the other two behind the subject to either side, one above the shoulders and one at chest height. At every moment the three outlines then box the body in from six evenly spread directions, and every part of it faces some phone. Studio's live views show one column per phone, its picture on top and its depth below, however many there are.

A session

  1. Calibrate (Studio's Rig page, once, kept until a phone moves). Measure where the phones are, with the calibration castle held by hand or the calibration tree standing on the turntable (below). About two minutes.
  2. Record the empty room (Rig page, kept with the calibration). Three seconds with nobody in any picture, lights and room as they'll be for the scan. This is the background the outlines are cut against, and it's worth several millimetres of accuracy.
  3. Scan. The subject steps on, facing phone A, arms a hand's width from their sides. Turn steadily: anything from about 20 to 60 seconds a turn works, so a motorised turntable at its usual speed is fine. Studio measures how far the subject has turned from the phones' depth and says when it passes 750° (two turns plus overlap). All the phones start together when you click; the recordings arrive on the Mac by themselves.
  4. Check. Studio shows whether the scan is good before they step off: how much they moved, how well the views agree, any part that wasn't seen.

Calibrating: castle or tree

Every phone works out where it is by looking at a target whose markers sit at known places. Studio takes a snapshot from all the phones at the same instant, a few times with the target in different places, and solves for every phone at once. Either target works with two phones or three.

The castle

Hold it still where the subject's chest will be and click Take snapshot (or press Space). Move it between snapshots: higher, lower, nearer, turned, tilted. A yellow box on each live view shows where it hasn't been yet. About eight snapshots.

The tree

With three phones around the turntable, whoever holds the castle is always in front of one of them. The tree stands on the turntable by itself and covers the height of a torso in every snapshot, and Studio takes the snapshots for you:

  1. Choose Use the calibration tree instead, stand the tree on the turntable, step back and press Start.
  2. When every phone that sees it has seen it stand still for a second and a half, Studio takes the snapshot and the Mac beeps.
  3. Pick it up, turn it 30–60°, set it down somewhere a little different and step back. Studio won't take the same spot twice.
  4. After six to eight placements a chime says the phones are measured to within 2 mm at the body, and the snapshots stop.

The tree's upper two segments can sit a fraction of a degree off their design on the trunk; the calibration measures that too, and takes it out.

Seeing the result

When calibration is done, Studio draws the rig in 3D: every phone where it was found, with its field of view and where it points, the target in each snapshot, and what each phone's LiDAR saw. Drag to turn it. It is a quick way to see that the phones are where you put them and look where you meant.

The first time you use the tree, calibrate with the castle first, then switch to the tree without touching the phones. Studio keeps the castle's answer and says how far apart the two put each phone: within about 2 mm, they agree.

What makes a good scan

Clothing and hair

  • Close-fitting, matte, mid-tone clothing, ideally what the garments will be fitted over. The form follows whatever they wear: loose fabric makes a loose form.
  • Tie hair up above the neck. Hair over the shoulders becomes part of the outline.
  • Arms a hand's width out. An arm against their side hides that side from the phones.

The room

  • Even light. No strong window or lamp directly behind them in either phone's view.
  • Contrast behind the subject. A background that isn't the colour of their skin or clothing gives the sharpest outlines. A plain dark or saturated backdrop behind each phone's view is the simplest fix where Studio reports weak contrast.
  • Leave the room alone after the empty-rig recording: same lights, same doors. Re-record it if anything changes.

What doesn't matter

  • Shadows, clutter and anything more than a quarter of a metre behind the subject: the LiDAR rules it out by distance.
  • People or pets moving a metre or more behind the subject. Doppelmaker notices them and ignores those frames' outlines where they cross.
  • Markers: optional. Small printed stickers on bony places can help tracking a featureless shape, but on a person the result is the same without them.

The person

Stand naturally, weight on both feet, eyes on a fixed point. Breathe gently and evenly. A little sway is expected and removed; a deep breath or a shifted foot is noticed and reported.

Hardware to buy

  • Two masts (three with a third phone): 3/4-inch EMT electrical conduit, about 1.8 m each, cut with a tubing cutter.
  • For the tree: one more length of the same conduit, about 1.5 m (1.8 m if it will also calibrate the phones raised for wig heads). Nothing else.
  • A board joining the two floor sockets, so the masts stay at right angles: 3/4-inch plywood ripped 200 mm (8 in) × 1.8 m, or a 2x8. A 2x6 works too; the sockets' 180 mm flanges overhang it a little, but their screws still land in it. A third mast needs its own base, for example a 600 mm square of the same plywood.
  • Eight wood screws (#10 × 3/4-inch flat-head) for the sockets.
  • A turntable rated for a person's weight, turned by hand or by motor, slowly and steadily.
  • Two right-angle USB-C cables and chargers.
