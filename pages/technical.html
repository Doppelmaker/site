Abstract

Doppelmaker builds a printable dress form or wig head from two or more LiDAR iPhones fixed on masts while the subject turns on a turntable. The phones' relative poses come from a printed 3D marker target (the hand-held castle or the free-standing calibration tree); gravity comes from ARKit; the turntable axis, the subject's sway and her shape are estimated jointly from the LiDAR; and the surface is then corrected by the colour cameras' outlines, which also estimate each phone's LiDAR range error. On the digital twin the finished torso lies 0.37 mm (median) and 1.26 mm (95th percentile) from the truth, and girths come within about 1 to 2 mm.

This paper walks through every step, with the parameters the code actually uses, and adds new numerical experiments run against the pipeline's own solver code. The main conclusions:

  • Calibration: at 1 px of corner noise (typical of real detections; renders give about 0.3), the tree reaches about 1 mm at the subject after 8 placements; the hand-held castle needs 12 well-varied snapshots to reach 2 mm, and a castle held in one spot never gets there. A completion rule of 6 placements accepts about a third of runs that are still above 2 mm; 8 placements does not. The reported error is honest on average but about 1.2× optimistic (1.8× at the 90th percentile).
  • Intrinsics are taken from ARKit as delivered, with a pinhole model. A focal-length error that is the same in calibration and scan barely moves a girth (it is absorbed); one that differs between them (focus moving between calibration and scan) moves a girth by about 9 mm per 1 %. Focal-length stability, not LiDAR resolution, is the largest sensitivity found.
  • The outline edge is placed at the halfway crossing of a colour difference computed on gamma-encoded pixels. On a blurred edge that point is not halfway in light, and it moves toward the darker side by up to about 0.45 of the blur width: about 1 to 4 mm of girth with a dark backdrop. A simulation that renders in linear light cannot show it.
  • Timing is not a bottleneck: clock sync, frame phase between phones and motion blur are all below half a pixel at the subject for 20 to 60 s per turn.
  • A higher-resolution LiDAR would help only the concave places no outline touches (under the bust, the small of the back). The LiDAR's true sampling is a few hundred dots, not 49,152 pixels, and because the phones never move, each row of dots sweeps the same horizontal line round the body on every turn. Between those lines the depth is interpolated by the phone, the same way every frame. A better colour camera is not needed: recording at the full 1920 px the phone already captures is free, and fixing the edge bias matters more than more pixels.
  • Layouts: 0/90 is the best two-phone layout by every measure here. With three phones, 0/120/240 is the only layout in which no phone has another inside its field of view; 0/90/235 puts two phones 17.5° off each other's axis.

How this paper was made

Where this paper quotes a parameter, it is the default in the source. Three kinds of number appear, and they are labelled:

  • Twin results: the digital twin, a parametric body turned on a simulated turntable in front of simulated phones, run through the full pipeline. Because the true shape is known, every error can be measured exactly.
  • New experiments: run for this paper by calling the pipeline's own calibration solver (snapshots.solve, with its bundle adjustment) on projected marker corners with controlled errors, plus small geometric models where stated. Each has a figure. They are cheaper and narrower than full twin runs: marker self-occlusion by the castle's own blocks and the tree's trunk is not simulated, so both targets look a little better here than they will in a room.
  • Estimates: arithmetic from stated assumptions, clearly marked.

1. The pipeline

The design rests on one observation: with the phones fixed, anything that moves in their depth is the subject. A hand-held phone cannot tell its own motion from hers; fixed phones can, so sway becomes something to estimate and remove instead of a reason to re-scan. On the twin, removing only the turntable's rotation leaves 6.1 / 23.7 mm of error (median / 95th percentile) on the raw points with 4 cm of lean; tracking the sway as well brings it to 0.5 / 2.1 mm.

2. Sensors and their geometry

2.1 The colour camera

The rig uses the main (wide, 24 mm-equivalent) camera through ARKit, which delivers 1920 × 1440 frames at 60 Hz with a pinhole intrinsic matrix K per frame. The phones stand in portrait, so the long side is vertical. With f ≈ 1450 px at 1920 (ARKit reports the exact value per frame and per phone; this paper uses 1450 throughout), the field is about 67° tall and 53° wide, and a pixel at distance D covers

wpx = D / f  ≈  0.69 mm at 1 m (1920 px),  1.38 mm at 1 m (960 px).

The phones record at most 960 px wide (Studio's rig_rgb_width), a 5× saving in data. Nothing is lost in frame count or colour, but edge position is another matter: Section 9 shows that a systematic outline error of one pixel costs 2π × 1.38 ≈ 8.7 mm of girth at 960 px and 4.3 mm at 1920.

2.2 The LiDAR

Apple calls it LiDAR; it is a flash time-of-flight depth camera. Nothing in it scans or moves: a VCSEL array behind a diffractive element projects a fixed grid of infrared dots all at once, and a SPAD sensor times each dot's return directly (direct ToF, unlike the phase-measuring indirect ToF cameras that give a distance at every pixel). ARKit delivers a 256 × 192 depth map (and a 0/1/2 confidence map) registered to the colour camera, at 60 Hz. That map is not 49,152 measurements. Teardown and IR-camera reports give 576 dots for the 12–14 Pro (64 emitters multiplied 3 × 3 by the diffractive element) and about 112 for the 15 Pro (an 8 × 14 pattern); the depth map is interpolated from those dots, guided by the colour image [1–3]. Spread evenly over the field, that is a dot every 2.5° (43 mm at 1 m) on the older phones and every 5.6° (98 mm) on the newer.

The fixed rig turns this into something specific. The only sweeping is done by the turntable. The dots are fixed in the phone, the phone is fixed in the room, and the subject turns about a vertical axis, so each row of dots traces the same horizontal line round her body on every turn. Along those lines the sampling is dense (the body sweeps past); between them, every frame's depth is the phone's interpolation, made the same way each time. Averaging 300 frames removes the LiDAR's random noise, but not an interpolation error that is identical in every frame. Sway and breathing move her a few millimetres vertically, far less than the 43–98 mm between rows.

Assumption The twin models the LiDAR as a dense 256 × 192 range image with white noise, a median filter, flying pixels at edges and an optional Gaussian low-pass. It has no dots. Every twin number about concave places (the underbust especially) therefore assumes the densifier behaves like a mild blur. A printed target with a narrow groove of known depth (12 mm) is the right probe of how it actually behaves.

The LiDAR's per-pixel noise in the twin is σ = 4 mm × range (an assumption, roughly in line with published characterisations of the sensor); the refinement weights LiDAR at 6 mm per metre of range to include its smoothing.

2.3 ARKit and the IMU

ARKit gives each frame a camera pose in its own world, a tracking state, and through that pose the direction of gravity in the camera frame. On a fixed phone the pose should never change; the pipeline uses ARKit for gravity only, because moving things in view can push ARKit's position estimate around while the phone stands still (on the twin's busy room, up to 1.0 mm for phones that never moved). "Did a phone move?" is decided from the phone's own gyroscope and accelerometer (Section 6.8). The IMU is recorded at 100 Hz on the same clock and used to report mast wobble.

3. Calibration

3.1 What has to be known, and what does not

Calibration estimates one thing: the rigid pose of every phone relative to a reference phone (A), six numbers per phone. Intrinsics are taken from ARKit (Section 4 questions that). "Up" is gravity. Nothing about the turntable, the floor or lens heights is measured: the turntable axis is found later from the subject's own rotation, and heights are measured from the subject (the neck base), not from a floor. This was a deliberate simplification and it holds up: nothing downstream needs a floor.

3.2 The two targets

Both are three-dimensional by design: a flat board has a well-known near-ambiguity in pose (a small tilt and a small shift look alike); blocks far apart in all three directions do not. Both use ArUco 4 × 4 markers 42 mm across, inlaid 2 mm deep in a second filament. Their geometry is taken as printed (±0.15 mm over 250 mm is a 0.06 % scale error), except that the tree's two upper segments each get a small pose correction (below), because they sit on a keyed cone joint that can have play.

3.3 Detection and per-view pose

Each phone's snapshot is searched for markers (OpenCV ArUco, dictionary 4 × 4_250, whose first 100 markers are the older 4 × 4_100 bit for bit). With two or more of the target's markers found, the target's pose in that camera comes from all their corners jointly: SQPnP for a global solution, then Levenberg–Marquardt refinement. If any marker's corner RMS exceeds max(3 × the median marker's, 1.5 px), it is dropped and the pose refitted once. A phone's view enters the solve only if its PnP RMS is below 2.5 px.

3.4 Chaining and the bundle adjustment

Initial phone poses come from chaining: for every snapshot that both A and phone c see, TA←c = TA←target Tc←target−1, averaged over snapshots (rotation mean on SO(3), arithmetic mean of translations), then propagated through phones already linked.

The bundle adjustment then minimises, over every marker corner Xk of every snapshot s in every phone c:

Σ ρ( ‖ π(Kc, TA←c−1 Qs Gg(k) Xk) − ucks ‖ )  +  Σg ‖ θg / 0.3° ‖² + ‖ tg / 1 mm ‖²

with unknowns the phone poses TA←c (A fixed: the gauge), one target pose Qs per snapshot, and for the tree a rigid correction Gg = (θg, tg) per upper segment, rotating about the segment's centre. ρ is the Huber loss with a 1 px scale (SciPy least_squares, trust-region reflective, Jacobian scaling). The segment prior makes one sigma of correction cost as much as one pixel of residual.

Justified The segment refinement is necessary, not decorative. With the upper segments 0.44° and a millimetre off their design (realistic play), taking the tree as designed puts the phones 4–7 mm wrong while the reported error still looks fine; refined, 0.4–0.5 mm, and the 0.44° is found. Holding the corrections near zero with a prior, rather than freeing them, keeps the target from absorbing the phones' error.

3.5 How good is it: misalignment and agreement

Studio reports two numbers after each snapshot. Misalignment comes from the Gauss–Newton covariance C = (JTJ)+ σ̂² of each phone's pose, expressed at the subject:

misalignmentc = √( σpos² + (σrot × 1.1 m)² )

Agreement is the largest change in any phone's pose (position plus rotation × 1.1 m) when one shared snapshot is left out and the problem re-solved (with the segment corrections frozen, for speed). Calibration is "done" when both are under 2 mm with at least 4 snapshots (castle) or 6 placements (tree). A snapshot whose joint RMS exceeds max(2 px, 3 × the median) is flagged for dropping.

3.6 New experiment: targets, counts and noise

The question the operator actually faces is how many snapshots, of which target, are enough. The pipeline's solver was run on projected marker corners with two noise levels: 0.3 px per corner (what the twin's renderer produces) and 1.0 px (typical of real marker detections). Two phones at 0/90, A at 1.0 m height and B at 1.55 m, both 1 m from the axis; 6 trials per cell; the tree turned in place with its segments 0.44° and 1 mm off; the castle either held all over the torso volume (±15 cm sideways, −35 to +30 cm in height, turned and tilted ±25°) or held in one spot.

Three things follow. Error scales almost linearly with corner noise, so the difference between rendered (0.3 px) and real (about 1 px) detections is the largest single factor, and its source matters (Section 4 suggests one). The tree's advantage is not convenience alone: its markers span 613 mm of height in every placement, and at 1 px it reaches in 8 placements what the castle needs 12 well-varied snapshots for. And a castle held in one spot is the worst case by some margin: variety matters more than count.

Tuning The tree's "done" threshold (6 placements, misalignment and agreement below 2 mm) accepted 2 of 6 two-phone runs and 3 of 6 three-phone runs that were truly above 2 mm at 1 px noise; at 8 placements, 0–1 of 6. Over all tree runs with 8 or more placements, the true error was 1.2× the reported misalignment at the median and 1.8× at the 90th percentile. A rule of 8 placements, or a reported misalignment below 2 / 1.8 ≈ 1.1 mm, makes "done" mean done.

Why is the covariance optimistic? It assumes independent, correctly scaled pixel errors and a correct model. Here the model is not correct (the segment corrections are held by a prior, the intrinsics are fixed), and the errors of one marker's four corners are not independent. The leave-one-out agreement catches some of what the covariance misses, which is why both are required.

3.7 Gravity, the look point and the rig frame

Each phone's ARKit gravity, rotated into A's frame by its calibrated pose, gives an estimate of up; the mean is used, and the spread between phones is checked (twin: 0.005°). The look point is the least-squares point nearest every phone's optical axis, Σ(I − ddT)x = Σ(I − ddT)o. The rig frame puts +Z along gravity, the origin 1 m below the look point (a datum only), +X horizontal toward A. The tracker later moves the origin onto the real turntable axis.

An up error of δ tilts every horizontal measuring plane by δ; a girth changes by about 1 − cos δ (0.015 % at 1°, negligible), but a landmark height 15 cm from the axis moves by 2.6 mm per degree. At 0.005° it is irrelevant.

4. The intrinsics nobody checks

Every projection in the pipeline (calibration, outlines, LiDAR back-projection) uses the pinhole K that ARKit attaches to the frame, with no lens distortion. The experiments below show where that model is safe and where it is not.

4.1 Focal length

The tree calibration was run with the solver given a focal length off by ε (−1 % to +1 %), then the girth of a 150 mm-radius vertical cylinder on the axis was rebuilt from the four outline planes of two phones, using the calibration it had just produced. Two cases: the same ε in calibration and scan, and a correct calibration followed by a scan with ε (as if focus moved between them and ARKit's K did not follow).

The result is a clean piece of geometry. An outline gives an angle, α ≈ r/D. A focal error scales every angle by 1/(1 + ε); the calibration, seeing a target that looks too small, places the phones (1 + ε) too far away. Radius = angle × distance, and the two factors cancel: 0.3 mm of girth per 0.5 %. The calibration's poses are wrong by about 15 mm per percent (the phones' baseline is scaled) and its reported misalignment does not notice at all, but the girth is fine. Change K between calibration and scan and nothing cancels: 9.4 mm per percent, the full proportional error.

Assumption If focus is locked only after the calibration snapshots, and the lens moves between calibration and scan and ARKit's per-frame K does not track it exactly, the girth carries the full fractional change. Focus breathing on phone lenses is typically a fraction of a percent, which is 1–3 mm of girth. The remedy is simple: every frame carries K, so f in the snapshots can be compared with f in the scan frames, and focus can be locked before calibrating, at the subject's distance, and held for the session.

Assumption The consistent-ε case has a second effect. LiDAR is metric and does not share the camera's angle error, so with the phones placed 7 mm too far per half-percent, each phone's LiDAR surface lands about ε × D away from where its outlines put the body. The refinement's per-phone "LiDAR reads long by x mm" (Section 9.3) would report that as a LiDAR error, and "correct" the LiDAR for it. A range error of a few millimetres per phone is as likely to be the camera's focal length as the LiDAR. A LiDAR check against the calibration target separates them: the printed target's faces are a known distance from its markers.

4.2 Lens distortion

The same experiment with radial distortion k1 added to the projected corners and outlines, and ignored by the solver:

Each 4 px of corner shift adds about 1 mm of pose error and 0.25 mm of girth, and the joint RMS rises only from 0.42 to 0.50 px at 16 px of shift: the bundle adjustment hides it. The torso sits near the middle of the picture, which is why girths suffer less than poses. Whether ARKit's frames are undistorted is not documented; AVFoundation exposes the lens's distortion table for its own capture, ARKit does not [5]. Apple's main cameras have small distortion, but "small" at 1920 px can easily be a few pixels at the corners, and the calibration target is often near the corners.

Assumption Real marker detections show about 1 px RMS per corner against a rendering's 0.3, and part of that may be distortion. Letting the bundle adjustment estimate one k1 (and optionally f) per phone, with priors, is well conditioned: the tree's placements cover the picture. It would also turn Section 4.1's silent pose error into a measured quantity.

5. Time

5.1 Clocks

Each phone keeps its own clock (ProcessInfo.systemUptime, the clock of ARFrame.timestamp and CoreMotion). Studio's clock is the Mac's monotonic clock. The phone measures the offset NTP-style: 15 exchanges 40 ms apart, keeping the one with the shortest round trip RTT, and

offset = tstudio − (tsend + treceive) / 2,    |error| ≤ RTT/2 (asymmetry)

It re-syncs every 15 s, and again right after each take; the take is stamped with that fresh offset, a single constant. The twin assumes 6 ms of error.

Minor A constant offset ignores drift between the phone's crystal and the Mac's. At a typical 20 ppm relative drift, a 2-minute take accumulates 2.4 ms, about 0.1 mm at the body (Figure 8). Interpolating between the offsets measured before and after the take removes it.

5.2 Starting together and the frame grid

Studio schedules every start 1.5 s ahead in its own time; each phone converts that to its clock, sleeps, and starts recording. ARKit delivers frames on a 60 Hz grid; the recorder keeps a frame when at least 1/f − 4 ms has passed since the last one kept. Two consequences:

  • The kept rate is 60 divided by a whole number. Studio's requested rate is honoured only when it divides 60: at 22 s a turn it asks for 8.2 Hz and gets 7.5; at 40 s it asks for 4.5 and gets 4.3. Harmless.
  • The phase between phones is set by when each one's first frame arrived after its wake-up (within one 16.7 ms frame plus scheduling jitter) and is then kept, until a frame is dropped by the writer, which shifts that phone's phase permanently by 16.7 ms. Over a long take the phases can wander.

5.3 How the tracker uses time

The tracker groups frames into slices: one frame per phone within one frame period. Before a slice is registered, each frame's points are rotated back about the axis by the turntable's speed times its offset from the slice's mean time, ω Δt. Sway is not compensated inside the slice, but afterwards every frame gets its own pose by interpolating the slice poses (spherical for rotation, linear for translation) at its own timestamp. Because the slice time is the mean of its frames' times, a linear sway is handled exactly to first order; what is left is the curvature of the sway over Δt, micrometres.

Justified Clock refinement from the rotation is off by default, correctly. At a steady ω, a clock error δ on phone c moves every one of its frames by a yaw of ω δ about the axis, which is exactly what a calibration error of phone c rotated about the turntable axis looks like. The two are separable only when ω varies a lot. On the twin, the refinement moved clocks 10–20 ms the wrong way; the studio's sync was already within a few ms.

Alternative Phase-locking the frames would remove the slice question entirely: each phone keeps the ARKit frame nearest to the studio-time grid k/f (it already knows the offset), instead of the first one after its own last frame. Frames from every phone would then lie within ±8 ms of each other for the whole take, dropped frames would not shift the phase, and the recorded rate would be exactly what Studio asked for. This is not per-phone triggering (each phone deciding from its own rotation estimate, which breaks the pairing); it is the opposite, a shared grid.

5.4 Motion blur, rolling shutter and the timestamp

ARKit runs the camera at 60 Hz, so an exposure is at most 1/60 s. Skin 15 cm from the axis moves 2πr/T: 43 mm/s at 22 s a turn, so 0.7 mm of smear in a 1/60 s exposure (half a pixel at 960 px). At the outline the edge itself moves much less: for an ellipse 330 × 230 mm the support function changes at most 50 mm per radian of turn, so the outline smears by at most 0.24 mm. A symmetric smear does not move a halfway crossing, provided the frame's timestamp is the middle of its exposure.

Rolling-shutter readout on recent Pro main cameras measures about 3–5 ms in video modes [4]. In portrait the sensor reads across the picture, so the two sides of her outline are exposed 2–3 ms apart: 0.1 mm.

Assumption Two timing facts are assumed, not known. Whether ARFrame.timestamp marks the start, middle or end of the exposure (an offset of up to 8 ms; the same on every phone only if their locked exposures are equal), and how the LiDAR depth of a frame is aligned in time with its colour image. The tracker poses the body from depth; the outlines come from colour. A 10 ms lag between them at 16°/s is a 0.16° yaw disagreement, 0.4 mm along the surface at the waist, and would show in the refinement's outline residual as an antisymmetric pattern (one edge in, the other out). It is worth looking for in residual maps.

Conclusion on timing. At 20–60 s a turn none of these is near the accuracy target. Timing is not what limits Doppelmaker, and a faster turntable (the twin at 22 s a turn did as well as at 40 s) costs nothing measurable. Calibration snapshots of the tree are immune by construction (it is still); a hand-held castle moves perhaps a few mm/s, times a capture spread of one frame (17 ms): well under 0.1 mm.

6. Separating her from the room

Every decision in the pipeline that looks at the room is either confined to her space or a vote no one part of the picture can win. The outlines are where this is hardest, because colour difference against an empty room says "not the empty room", which is true of her, of a passer-by, of a cat and of a ceiling fan.

6.1 The empty room

Three seconds of the empty rig, taken once per calibration, with exposure and white balance locked. The background is the per-pixel median (anything passing through for less than half the take is not in it). Each frame is first brought to the median's gain per channel, then the per-pixel spread is 1.4826 × the median absolute colour deviation: a robust standard deviation. Pixels where the room itself changes (a window, a screen, a fan) get a large spread and are trusted less later.

6.2 The colour difference

For a scan frame, a gain per channel is estimated as the median of per-pixel ratios over pixels known to be room (outside the gate); the median of ratios rather than the ratio of medians, because the latter is badly biased on a two-tone room (15 % on the twin). The distance is the Euclidean RGB distance between the frame and the gain-corrected background, in 8-bit units. Where the empty room had spread, the distance is scaled down by τ / max(τ, 2 × spread).

Tuning The constants of the segmentation (τ = 6 × the median room distance clipped to 8–40, seeds at 1.8 τ, growth to 0.7 τ, pieces of at least 0.2 % of the picture, a 3 % dilation of the gate, 3 × 3 opening and 5 × 5 closing) are empirical rather than derived from a noise model. They matter less than they seem, because the sub-pixel edge (below) does not use τ when the contrast is good; they decide which pixels are hers, not where the edge is. The empty-room take's spread map is the natural source of a noise model for them.

6.3 Placing the edge

The mask's outer contour (every second pixel, smoothed with σ = 2 px along the contour) gives points and outward normals. Along each normal the colour distance is sampled from −3 to +3 px in quarter pixels; the edge is placed where it crosses halfway between the mean inside (beyond 1.5 px in) and the mean outside, interpolated linearly, and the move is clipped to ±2 px. Crossing the detection threshold instead would put the edge half a pixel outside on a blurred edge, +0.7 mm on every girth; with the halfway rule the twin's measured bias is −0.2 px on A and 0.0 px on B.

But "halfway" is measured on gamma-encoded pixel values (JPEG from the phone's sRGB-like video encoding). A real edge is blurred optically, by the lens, the demosaic and the motion, in linear light: a pixel on the edge holds α of her light and 1 − α of the room's. Encoding is concave, so the encoded halfway point is not α = 0.5:

A light subject on the recommended dark backdrop (ratio 5–15) gets an outline 0.3–0.45 σ outside her; a dark garment against a light wall, 0.25–0.35 σ inside. With an effective blur σ of 0.6–1.0 px at 960 px, that is 0.2–0.45 px, and at 8.7 mm of girth per pixel, about 1.5–4 mm of girth, the same sign everywhere the contrast has the same sign. Recording at 1920 px does not help: the optical blur is the same width in millimetres.

Assumption A twin that renders in linear light and adds noise measures an edge bias (−0.2 / 0.0 px) that contains none of this. The remedy is to convert the frame and the background to linear light (invert the sRGB curve, a lookup table) before the colour distance. The JPEG's 4:2:0 chroma subsampling and the phone's sharpening halo are further edge effects; a target with black and white quadrants on one drum (dark on light and light on dark at the same radius) shows the sign flip directly.

6.4 Confidence and flags

Each outline point gets a confidence (inside − outside) / (2.5 τ), clipped to 0–1, and a flag: 1 if within 3 px of the picture's border (cut off, not her edge), 2 if the colour is still "not room" outside the mask (the gate cut her, e.g. legs), 3 if the edge lies within 4 px of a vetoed or out-of-band region while the colour beyond is not the room (an intrusion). Flagged points are not used. Points whose room, 4 px outside, was itself changing in the empty take keep a fifth of their confidence.

6.5 Without an empty room

The LiDAR mask alone sits about 5 px inside her (its rim pixels are mixed and get no confidence): −25 mm on every girth on the twin, worse than no outlines. The fallback is GrabCut on the colour image, seeded by the LiDAR (eroded LiDAR = surely her, the gate ring = probably room), with the edge at the colour-gradient peak and half the trust. It halves the LiDAR's error but is about 1 px; the empty room is worth 3–4 mm of girth.

6.6 The person mask

Every frame also carries the phone's own person segmentation (Vision, on the Neural Engine). It is an independent cue for the case the depth veto cannot handle: someone within a metre of the turntable.

6.7 Mirrors, windows, screens

Handled by placement, not software: a mirror wall diagonally behind both phones is outside the ±26.5° horizontal field of each if every phone faces within about 63° of straight away from it.

6.8 Did a phone move? Its own sensors decide

The calibration holds only while every phone stays exactly where it was. The room in front of a rig phone is not controlled (a convention floor has people walking past by the hundred), so this question is never answered from the pictures, or from ARKit's world tracking, which the scene also drives. Each phone answers it from its own gyroscope and accelerometer, which nothing in the scene can move.

The method is detection, not integration. A fixed phone's rotation rate is its noise and a slowly varying bias (a running mean while quiet, subtracted), and its acceleration is gravity. Anything that touches the rig shows as a burst far above that floor. The phone runs device motion at 100 Hz continuously, camera standby included, and from the moment a calibration begins (its first snapshot) counts as follows:

  • Quiet: keep the mean acceleration of the last two seconds, which is the gravity direction in the phone's frame. Nothing is integrated.
  • Event: a burst starts one: the lightly smoothed rotation rate above 0.6°/s, or acceleration departing from its own half-second mean by more than 0.012 g (vibration, not tilt). During the event, and only then, the bias-corrected rotation rate is integrated.
  • Settled: quiet again for 0.8 s, then 2 s more for the gravity direction after.

The event's net rotation has two parts. Tilt (pitch and roll) is the angle between the gravity directions before and after: no integration at all, and with two-second means of an accelerometer whose per-sample noise is about 1.5 mg, good to about 0.01°. Turn about the vertical (yaw) is invisible to gravity; it is the component along gravity of the gyro integrated over the event alone, seconds at most, so bias and noise contribute about a hundredth of a degree. A mast that rings and settles back where it was (someone stepping onto the turntable, the floor bouncing under passers-by) nets out near zero: under 0.04° it is counted as a bounce and tolerated, however many there are. Anything larger is a shift, and shifts add up: 0.06° is "check", 0.10° (about 2 mm at the subject) is "moved", and scanning stops until the rig is calibrated again. Each event is measured against the two seconds just before it, so a slow drift of the accelerometer's own bias (a milli-g over a warm-up would be 0.06°) never accumulates.

What a burst detector cannot see is slow creep below its threshold, such as a clamp slipping over minutes; a compression-gland clamp is not expected to. What the sensors did not observe (the app not running), they do not vouch for: a gap in the motion stream, or a phone that was not counting when the calibration began, is reported as such rather than as "still". The pipeline runs the same detector over every take's recorded IMU stream and fails a take in which a phone turned.

The pictures still give a second, independent reading: each phone's picture is compared with its calibration snapshot by phase correlation in 4 × 3 tiles, and the median tile shift is reported. It is information only. Besides depending on the room, a median of tile translations cannot see roll (a turn about the lens axis moves the tiles in a pinwheel), and its natural thresholds (2 px at 960 px is 0.16°, three times the calibration's accuracy) are coarser than the inertial measurement.

7. Tracking the turning body

7.1 Points

From each depth frame: every second pixel with confidence 2, back-projected, with normals from central differences, rejecting pixels next to a depth jump over 3 cm. Points within 0.6 m of the provisional centre (the look point's vertical) and higher than 0.7 m below the aim point are kept, voxel-averaged at 5 mm.

7.2 Pass 1: in time order

For each slice: predict the pose by the turntable speed so far (a linear fit to the last eight yaws); search yaw about the predicted pose, ±25° for the first slices and ±12° after, coarse at 3° and then at 1° around the best, scoring by the mean distance (capped at 15 mm) from a thousand of the slice's points to the shape so far; refine with point-to-plane ICP against the shape, coarse to fine at 30, 12 and 6 mm correspondence limits, with Tukey weights (zero beyond 10 mm); add the slice to the shape. After one turn the shape closes on itself. The shape is a voxel-hashed sum of points and normals, so adding a slice costs only that slice.

ICP ends at a step under 10 µm or when its weighted RMS has not improved for eight iterations, keeping the best pose seen: on a 5 mm voxel model the energy is piecewise, and the yaw of a near-cylinder otherwise wanders at 0.1–0.4 mrad per step without improving.

7.3 The turntable axis

Sixty of the pass-1 poses are taken as rotations about one axis. The direction is the weighted mean of the relative rotations' axes; a point on it solves (I − Rk)p = tk in least squares. Only the position is used; up stays gravity. A standing person balances to gravity whatever the turntable does, and the fitted direction is biased by sway (0.4° off on the twin with 4 cm of lean), so a tilted turntable is treated as more sway. The frames are moved onto the found axis and pass 1 is run again. On the twin the axis was 36 mm from where the phones aimed.

Justified Using gravity for the axis direction is a good argument, not a convenience: the body, not the turntable, defines the measuring frame, and the body's mean posture is upright.

7.4 Passes 2 and 3

Rebuild the shape from every frame (3.5 mm voxels) with the current poses, re-register every slice to it independently (in parallel), then smooth the trajectory: the unwrapped yaw, and separately the residual sway (rotation vector and translation), each with a Gaussian of σ = 0.25 s (half power near 0.75 Hz; sway is below 0.5 Hz). Three such passes, then the mean tilt is removed so the body frame is her mean posture, upright. The translation is kept, so the body frame's axis stays the turntable's.

7.5 What it reports

Turns, speed, sway at 1.3 m (95th percentile, maximum) and shape change: per slice, the median point-to-plane distance of its points to the shape built from the other phones. After rigid motion is removed that is what genuinely changed shape (breathing, arms, hips) plus noise. On the twin: body motion 2.1 / 3.5 mm (median / p95) recovered, shape change 1.1 mm p95 (the lean was rigid).

Observability is the weak point of any turntable tracker: yaw about a near-cylinder is held only by the body's departure from a circle. Two phones help (they see different departures at once); on the twin the yaw uncertainty after refinement was 0.10° at the 95th percentile, with or without stickers. A 0.1° yaw error is 0.26 mm along the surface at the waist and does not change a girth to first order.

8. Fusion and the first surface

8.1 TSDF fusion

With every frame posed in the body frame, the depth is fused into a truncated signed distance field (Open3D's voxel-block grid): 4 mm voxels, truncation 4 voxels (16 mm), depth up to 2.5 m, confidence 2 only, and only pixels whose back-projection lies inside the subject's cylinder. The fused points come out of a multithreaded hash map in a different order on every run, which would make the same recording give slightly different parts; they are sorted into a canonical order, and every stage from here on is byte-repeatable.

Each frame enters with weight 1, whatever its range and whatever the angle at which it sees the surface. Every LiDAR sample's noise grows with range and with grazing incidence, and the twin's phones stood at 1.0 and 1.5 m; a range- and incidence-weighted integration would be the textbook choice. It matters little here, because the refinement (Section 9) re-weights LiDAR by range and the outlines carry the convex shape.

The stage also reports a motion index: each frame's median distance to the fused surface (twin: 4.0–4.3 mm for protocol-following captures; it warns at 4.8, a threshold calibrated on the twin).

8.2 The closed surface

Screened Poisson reconstruction, depth 9 with a 1.1 bounding-box scale. Over the 1.4–1.5 m of subject above the crop, that is an octree cell of about 3.1 mm, finer than the 4 mm TSDF and the 5 mm LiDAR pixel, so Poisson is not the resolution limit. It runs single-threaded first (the multithreaded build is not repeatable run to run), in a child process so a C++ abort cannot take the stage down, with an implicit-surface fallback. The result is repaired to one closed 2-manifold (a permissively licensed closing step) and cut below the datum.

Every vertex is then given a support distance, its distance to the nearest real fused point. A vertex more than 5 mm from any data is surface Poisson invented. Over the torso band (50–82 % of standing height) more than 1 % invented warns and more than 5 % fails; the twin is under 0.2 %.

9. Outlines and the joint refinement

The LiDAR surface averages away its random noise but keeps its biases: a range offset on skin, a per-phone range scale, the densifier's blur. With assumed but plausible values (2 mm on skin, ±0.3 % scale, 1.5 px blur), the LiDAR-only twin torso came out −11 to −13 mm on every girth. The colour outlines do not share those errors, and the refinement uses them to fix the surface.

9.1 What an outline says

For outline point u with image normal n, the ray is r = K−1(u, 1) and the in-image tangent direction t = K−1(−ny, nx, 0); the plane normal is m = r × t, oriented outward, through the camera centre: m · x = o. On the current mesh, for every edge whose endpoints' normals face the camera on one side and away on the other (the sign of N · (V − C) changes), the point where it changes is interpolated linearly along the edge. Each such generator point is matched to its nearest outline point (within 10 px), and per outline point the outermost one is kept.

Justified Two details are each worth about 1 mm of radius (6 mm of girth). Using the nearest vertex instead of the edge crossing: vertices are about 1 cm apart on the 15k-vertex working mesh, and a vertex 5 mm behind the true generator, pushed out to the plane, inflates the form. And matching the wrong way round (outline point → vertices within a radius): on a concave stretch such as the waist, a vertex 10 px along the outline lies outside that point's tangent line and shrinks the form.

9.3 The LiDAR's range error, and an identifiability problem

Concave places (under the bust, the small of the back) never lie on an outline: there the surface is the LiDAR's. To carry the outlines' authority there, the refinement estimates a range offset and scale per phone where outlines and LiDAR both see her, and removes them everywhere. On the twin it found phone A 5.6 mm long at 1.1 m (truth about 5.3) and took the underbust from −2.6 to −0.2 mm. Phone B, which sees less outline-covered surface, came out +0.6 against a truth of −1.3.

Identifiability The two columns of the range-error model, 1 and range, correlate at 0.999 over the ranges a torso presents. The data fix one combination, the error at about 0.92 m; the split between offset and scale is set by the priors (5 mm against 1 %, which at 0.92 m are about equal), so the scale reported is the prior's choice. It does no harm at the torso's own ranges, which is all that is corrected. But the diagnostic "phone A: LiDAR reads long" in millimetres at 1.1 m is extrapolated, and a scale term only becomes meaningful with targets at different ranges. One offset per phone, reported at the torso's mean range, says exactly what the data support. And, from Section 4.1, interpret that offset as "LiDAR or camera".

Assumption One offset per phone assumes the LiDAR's error is the same everywhere on her. Subsurface scattering differs between skin and fabric, dark and light, and the error at a densifier's interpolation between dot rows (Section 2.2) depends on the local shape, which is worst exactly in the concave places this correction exists to fix. The diagnostics report the residual by region (6 heights × 8 directions), which is where a departure from one number per phone shows.

9.4 Breathing

Two fixed shape modes: radial expansion (outward-facing surface only, not limbs) in Gaussian bands centred at 73 % (chest) and 60 % (belly) of the mesh's height, σ = 5 % of the height. Each slice gets a weight per mode; the output is the mean breath. On the twin with deep breathing (8 mm), the chest swing was measured at 11.7 mm peak to peak and flagged, and the bust error fell from +4.7 to +3.3 mm.

Tuning The mode centres are fractions of the mesh's height, and the mesh's height depends on how much of her the crop includes (legs, the head). A data-driven alternative is cheap: the per-slice shape-change residual from Section 7.5, decomposed over time (its two leading components), gives this subject's own breathing modes. Alternatively, the bands can be placed from the landmarks (bust and waist heights) rather than from the mesh's extent.

9.5 Girth from the outlines alone

For a convex cross-section, Cauchy's formula gives the perimeter directly from the support function h(θ) (the distance from a centre to the tangent line in direction θ):

L = ∫02π h(θ) dθ

and a tape is the convex hull's perimeter, so this is exactly what a tape reads. Every near-vertical outline plane touching the torso near a height gives one sample of h at that height, and over a turn the samples cover every direction. The diagnostics compute this "outline-only tape" at every height, independently of the surface. On the twin it agreed with the truth to about 2 mm where the arms were clear of the sides.

Two corollaries of the formula. A uniform error δ in where outlines sit changes every girth by 2πδ: that is the 8.7 mm per pixel at 960 px used throughout. And it says which calibration errors matter:

  • Sideways errors cancel to first order. If phone B's pose is off sideways (or in yaw), one of its outline edges moves out and the other in by the same amount. Over a turn both edges sample every direction, so h(θ) gains +e from one edge's samples and −e from the other's: the girth is unchanged; the surface is less sharp and the residual shows "phones disagree".
  • Range errors do not. If B is e too far from the body, its outline's angle is right but it is multiplied by the wrong distance: δr ≈ r e / D, 0.15 mm per millimetre, about 1 mm of girth per millimetre of range error at 15 cm radius and 1 m. This is why 1–2 mm of calibration error turns into well under 2 mm of girth.

10. Closing and measuring

10.1 The body frame

Z is gravity; the origin is below the torso's axis (fitted on 30–60 % of the height range); Y is anterior, from the scan's start (the subject faces phone A) and then refined by fitting the mirror-symmetry plane: candidate planes containing up are scored by reflecting 8,000 surface samples and taking the trimmed distance to the surface, over yaw and lateral offset (grid search, then simplex, then an exact polish). Roll about the anterior axis is fitted separately and reported as lean (warn 2°, fail 6°).

10.2 Landmarks

From shape alone, as proposals the editor can override: neck base (where the cross-section stops shrinking going up), armpits (where the arm's section separates from the torso, the longest separated run), waist (minimum tape girth below the armpits), bust (maximum anterior extent), underbust (maximum curvature of the anterior profile below the bust), high hip (waist − 10 cm by dressmaking convention), hip (maximum tape girth below that), the shoulder line and the shoulder edge for the arm cut.

The underbust is the fragile one: on the twin its height moved 16–19 mm for shape changes under 1 mm, and on refined surfaces the ~1 mm texture of the refinement moved it by up to 13 mm. For that reason, named girths are not reported. A Measure tool slides and tilts a plane through the shape and reads a tape there, and the cut planes do not depend on the underbust line.

10.3 Trim, caps and the solid

The surface is cut at the neck, arm and hip planes; the largest component is kept and repaired; it is smoothed with Taubin's λ|μ filter (no shrinkage) for five iterations with the planar caps pinned. The stage checks that cleanup did not change the shape: volume within 0.3 % (warn) and 1 % (fail), girths at standard heights within 1 and 2.5 mm, and that no more than 3 % (warn) or 10 % (fail) of the kept surface is more than 5 mm from any depth sample.

Solids for printing follow four construction rules (material boundaries before cuts; never a boolean along a shared surface; snap vertices within 1 µm onto a plane before cutting by it; cutting tools with no vertex in their own axis planes), each enforced by one primitive and tested. They exist because a valid manifold in the geometry kernel can still weld into open edges in an STL; with them every exported part welds clean, with no repairs.

10.4 What a girth is

A tape bridges hollows, so a girth is the perimeter of the convex hull of the section's torso loop (arms are separate loops and excluded), after a moving-average smoothing of the loop over 10 mm of arc: a hull rides the outermost noise peaks and reads long, which a tape, which cannot follow millimetre bumps, does not. The surface's own perimeter is reported too.

11. Where to put the phones

The turntable means every phone sees every part of her over a turn, whatever the layout. Layout decides what is seen at the same instant (which is what separates rigid sway from real shape change, breathing, and a moved arm), how squarely the LiDAR sees each surface, and whether the phones see each other.

11.1 Two phones: why 0/90, not 0/60 or 0/120

Phone i at azimuth φi bounds the section in directions φi ± 90°. Two phones give four directions; they are evenly spaced (every 90°) only when the phones are 90° apart. At 60° or 120° the four directions bunch into pairs with a 120° gap between them: a third of the section unbounded at any instant. The same holds for the instantaneous pose: the uncertainty of her horizontal position in its worst direction, from LiDAR and outlines together on a 330 × 230 mm section, is lowest at 90° and about 35 % higher at 60° or 120° (Figure 14, right). At 180° the two outlines are the same two lines (no new information) and the LiDARs face each other.

That said, the pose term is not decisive: in every sensible layout her instantaneous position is pinned far below a millimetre, because each phone alone sees surface normals spread over about ±80°. The outline gap is what decides, and 0/90 is also the easiest to calibrate (both phones see the target's faces at 45°) and leaves room to step on. It is the right choice.

11.2 Three phones: 0/120/240 against 0/90/235

Two measures, and one constraint:

  • Outline gap: 0/120/240 gives six directions exactly 60° apart (a hexagon round every section at every instant). 0/90/235 gives 0, 90, 145, 180, 270, 325: gaps of 90, 55, 35, 90, 55 and 35°.
  • Worst LiDAR incidence, half the largest gap between phones: 60° for 0/120/240, 72.5° for 0/90/235.
  • Seeing each other. A phone looking at the axis sees another phone Δ degrees round the circle at (180° − Δ)/2 off its own axis. The portrait half-field is 26.5°. At 120° apart that is 30°, just outside. 0/90/235 has the 90° and 235° phones 145° apart: each sees the other 17.5° off axis, well inside the picture, mast, phone and LiDAR emitter included. Its other pair (125° apart) is at 27.5°, on the edge.

The map makes the case plainly. 0/120/240 is the unique best on both measures, and it sits in a small island (about ±7°) where no phone sees another; almost every other spread-out layout puts a phone inside a neighbour's picture. 0/60/120 matches it on outlines (the same three lines, since a phone and its opposite give the same outline directions) but sees one side of her only. 0/90/180 adds nothing over 0/90 for outlines and faces two LiDARs. 0/90/235 is worse than 0/120/240 on every measure and is the one layout here that is clearly inside the mutual-visibility zone. The masts at 0/120/240 are 1.73 m apart at a 1 m radius, against 1.41 m at 0/90. But 30° against a 26.5° half-field is only 3.5° of margin: a phone moved 6 cm round the circle closes it, so placement matters.

11.3 Heights

Placement in height is decided by viewing angle, not coverage: at 0.8–1.4 m every portrait phone covers hips to neck for subjects from 5′0″ to 6′4″. B at 5 cm above the shoulders sees their tops edge-on (−3°), where LiDAR returns are sparse; at 15–25 cm above, −12° to −16°. A stays at or below the navel to look up into the fold under the bust. Section 2.2 adds one more reason to keep the phones at different heights: their LiDAR rows land at different heights on her, so between them they sample more lines of the body.

12. What would actually help

A higher-resolution LiDAR? Only for the concave places, and only to the extent the densifier misbehaves there. Convex girths are set by the outlines, not the LiDAR; after refinement the LiDAR's role on convex surfaces is a weak backstop. Within the LiDAR, resolution is set by the dot count and the fixed rows of Section 2.2, not by the 256 × 192 map. The useful comparisons are an older 12–14 Pro (576 dots, against about 112) at the same spot, and phones at more heights, before anything more exotic.

A better colour camera? No. The phones already capture 1920 px and the rig throws half of it away; restoring it halves every random outline error and costs only storage. But the largest outline error is likely systematic (the gamma edge bias), and it is fixed in software, not by pixels. Lens distortion and the focal length's stability matter more, and both are calibration, not hardware.

Better timing? No. Every timing error is under half a pixel at the subject at 20–60 s a turn (Figure 8). Phase-locking frames to a shared grid is worth doing for tidiness, not accuracy.

A third phone? Yes, at 0/120/240, for what it adds at each instant (breathing and arm movement are separated from shape better with six outline directions), for the 1.5× more observations, and for a third set of LiDAR rows at a third height. Its calibration costs a little (Figure 4: the worse of two phones), and the tree handles it.

Better calibration? The current one is good enough if run with the tree and 8 or more placements: about 1 mm at 1 px, and range errors are what reach the girth (Section 9.5). What would make it better is self-knowledge: intrinsics and distortion estimated rather than assumed, and a stricter "done".
